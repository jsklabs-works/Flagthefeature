The New Payments Platform and Fast Settlement Service

An interactive walk through Alexandra Rush and Riaan Louw's article. It covers why Australia built real-time payments, how the NPP and the Reserve Bank's FSS work together, how banks manage liquidity around the clock, and what happened in the first six months.

“The NPP enables customers of financial institutions to make immediate payments 24 hours a day, every day of the week (‘24/7’). In conjunction with the development of the NPP, the Reserve Bank developed new infrastructure, the Fast Settlement Service (FSS), which provides for settlement of NPP transactions between financial institutions on a 24/7 basis across their Exchange Settlement Accounts (ESAs) at the Reserve Bank.”

Play 01 · Background

Why Australia needed fast payments

Before the NPP, obligations between institutions from cheque, card and direct entry (DE) payments settled “on a deferred basis periodically throughout the day in aggregated batches” in RITS. Funds could take “between a few hours and a day in the case of DE transactions”, and “as long as a few days for cheques”. Weekends and public holidays extended these times.

From 2010 to 2012 the Payments System Board (PSB) ran a Strategic Review of Innovation in the Payments System. It found gaps in retail payments and set four strategic objectives. Select an objective to see how the NPP answers it.

From review to industry utility
  • The PSB asked industry to find the best solution and endorsed a set of Core Criteria for assessing proposals (RBA 2012b).
  • Under APCA (now the Australian Payments Network), the Real-Time Payments Committee finalised an industry proposal in February 2013.
  • NPP Australia Limited (NPPA) was set up with 13 members (12 ADIs and the Reserve Bank) to develop and run the NPP as an industry utility.
International context

The PSB foresaw that without collaboration, Australian retail payment services “were at risk of falling behind those available in other countries”. Japan, Korea, Mexico and Switzerland had long had fast retail payments, and many more countries were building or upgrading them.

Play 02 · NPP and FSS projects

Building the NPP and the FSS

The NPP needed ADIs to exchange individual payment messages and FSS settlement instructions in real time, and to post to customer accounts within a few seconds. The program ran in phases. Select a milestone.

Inside the ADIs

Participants ran large internal projects to upgrade payments processing and monitoring, internet banking and mobile apps.

Inside the Reserve Bank

Besides building the FSS, the Bank introduced 24/7 shift work, upgraded building infrastructure for continuous operations, and worked with participants on liquidity for overnight and weekend settlement when the cash market is closed.

The RBA as a participant

A separate Banking Department project let the Bank join the NPP itself, so government agencies can offer faster payments, such as emergency benefit payments by the Department of Human Services.

Play 04 · How are NPP payments processed?

An NPP payment in six steps

For customers it takes moments. Behind it are clearing steps (the payer ADI confirms funds, the payee ADI confirms the account can receive NPP payments) and settlement steps (funds move between the ADIs), in quick succession.

Not routed through the FSS

Intrabank NPP transactions, and transactions between identified institutions that use the same sponsor, are not settled through the FSS.

Play 05 · How are NPP payments settled by the FSS?

Settle or reject, in about 50 milliseconds

Each NPP payment is settled with finality “by simultaneously crediting and debiting ESAs held at the Reserve Bank”. The FSS reuses some RITS infrastructure (such as the user interface) but settles NPP payments independently of the RITS System Queue. Each institution splits its single ESA into an FSS Allocation and a RITS Allocation, and NPP payments settle using the FSS Allocation only.

Why real-time settlement matters

Because each payment settles with finality immediately, the FSS “eliminates credit risk”. ADIs can make funds available to customers without generating credit risk, and without other controls such as caps on exposures.

Play 06 · Liquidity management

Keeping the FSS Allocation funded, 24/7

Institutions are encouraged to always keep enough funds in the FSS. With the FSS came a new RITS tool: upper and lower trigger points on the FSS Allocation. When one is reached, funds move automatically between the two allocations back to a reset point set by the institution. Try it below, modelled on the article's Figure 2.

RITS hours vs FSS hours

RITS settles between 7.30 am and 10 pm on business days, while the FSS settles NPP transactions 24/7, “so all ESA funds are transferred to the FSS Allocation when RITS is not operating”.

Intraday repos

Eligible institutions can use the Bank's Standing Facilities. Intraday repurchase agreements carry no interest charge but must be reversed later that day.

Open repos

Offered to institutions that would otherwise regularly need intraday repos, and to those settling DE or FSS payments when the cash market is closed (overnight or weekends). Open repos have no fixed maturity date.

The cash market is “the market where banks borrow and lend funds held in their ESAs, generally for one business day and on an unsecured basis” (footnote 2).

Play 08 · Operating principles, risk and resilience

Designed for continuous operation

The NPP and FSS were built for real-time payments and continuous operations, with high standards for availability, capacity and security. The Reserve Bank targets 99.995 per cent availability for the FSS. Pick a scenario to see which design features apply.

For the payments system as a whole
  • A new way to make retail payments adds resilience.
  • Because it is an RTGS system, the NPP could, if required, be used as an alternative to traditional ways of making wholesale interbank payments through RITS.
A changed risk profile

Recipients face fewer delays in receiving funds, but real-time behaviour monitoring becomes more important, “as there is no delay for the recipient of a fraudulent payment to access the proceeds”. Each ADI is responsible for its own fraud monitoring.

For more on resilience features, risks and mitigants, the article points to Box D of the Reserve Bank's April 2018 Financial Stability Review.

Play 09 · Activity post launch

The first six months

About 50 institutions launched in February 2018; by the end of August more than 65 ADIs had rolled out services. Volumes were deliberately constrained in the first weeks while institutions fine-tuned systems, and ADIs introduced services, channels and customer segments in stages. Adoption was gradual but sustained: over 1.9 million PayID registrations and, through the FSS, 19.4 million interbank transactions worth $15.1 billion to the end of August.

Play 10 · Summary

What it adds up to

“The NPP and FSS represent a significant development in the Australian payment system, with customers of financial institutions now having the ability to make immediate payments on a 24/7 basis with more easily remembered PayIDs. With the capacity to send detailed information along with these fast payments, the NPP has been designed to meet the needs of Australia's digital economy and has the potential to promote further innovation and competition in payments services in the years to come.”
Summary

Key takeaways

Clearing is decentralised

Payment gateways sit in each ADI's data centre; each ADI owns its speed, capacity and fraud monitoring.

Settlement is central and final

The FSS settles each payment gross, in central bank money, in about 50 ms, so payees can be credited without credit risk.

Liquidity is automated

Trigger and reset points move ESA funds between the FSS and RITS Allocations; all funds sit in the FSS when RITS is closed.

Innovation sits on top

Overlay services like Osko can launch without every participant joining, encouraging competition.

Later developments include the merger of NPP Australia with BPAY Group and eftpos to form Australian Payments Plus (2022), and the launch of PayTo, an NPP service for payer-authorised payment agreements. See the related plays in the payments playbook ↗.

Concepts explained

The ideas behind the article

Five ideas the article relies on but doesn't stop to explain. Each tab has a short explanation and something to try. Material that comes from general payments knowledge rather than the article is labelled.

ESA: an Exchange Settlement Account

An ESA is a bank's account at the Reserve Bank. Your bank account holds money your bank owes you. An ESA holds money the Reserve Bank owes the bank: central bank money.

Banks use their ESAs to pay each other. When you send $250 from Bank A to a friend at Bank B, Bank A's ESA goes down $250 and Bank B's goes up $250. That transfer is what actually moves the money between banks.

Mainly ADIs hold ESAs. Others settle through a sponsor that has one. For the NPP, each institution divides its single ESA into an FSS Allocation (NPP payments, 24/7) and a RITS Allocation (everything else, in RITS hours). See play 6.

Is an ESA a suspense account?

No. They're different but connected. The ESA lives at the Reserve Bank. A suspense account is a temporary holding account inside the bank's own books.

Overlay services

The NPP Basic Infrastructure is the plumbing: a fast messaging network, payment gateways, PayID addressing and real-time settlement through the FSS.

An overlay service is a commercial payment product built on top of that plumbing, with its own rules, message flows or customer experience. In the article's words, overlays “use the NPP Basic Infrastructure's capabilities to send payment messages quickly, address payments using PayIDs and send detailed information with payments”.

They range from simple industry standards to new message flows or payment types, and an overlay can launch without every participant joining. That gets new services to market faster and allows competition on shared infrastructure.

Analogy

The NPP is the road network. Overlay services are the delivery companies that use the roads, each with its own vehicles, prices and promises to customers.

Examples
  • Osko by BPAY, the first overlay (from the article): fast payments with data, 24/7, in digital banking; usable by corporates and fintechs.
  • PayTo (later, not in the article): payer-approved payment agreements, the NPP's replacement for direct debit.

Clearing vs settlement

Every interbank payment has two separate parts.

In an NPP payment (from the article)
  1. Clearing: Bank A sends the payment details. Bank B checks the account exists and can receive NPP payments, then confirms.
  2. Settlement: Bank A's gateway sends a settlement request. The FSS checks A's FSS Allocation, then debits A's ESA and credits B's at the same moment, in about 50 ms.
  3. Then Bank B credits the payee.

Why the gap matters

For cards and BECS, clearing happens through the day but settlement happens later, in net batches. In that gap, one bank is effectively trusting another to pay. The NPP removes the gap by settling every payment immediately and individually, which is why the article says the FSS “eliminates credit risk” and why the payee's bank can release funds within seconds.

RTGS vs DNS

RTGS · Real-Time Gross Settlement
  • Real-time: each payment settles when it's processed.
  • Gross: each payment settles on its own for its full amount, with no offsetting.
  • Final straight away across ESAs. (Footnote 1 of the article: payments across central bank accounts on a continuous gross basis.)
DNS · Deferred Net Settlement
  • Deferred: payments accumulate and settle at set times.
  • Net: each bank's obligations are offset and only the overall difference moves.
  • The article describes cheques, cards and direct entry settling this way: “in aggregated batches” in RITS.

Context · general knowledge, not from the article

Australia moved high-value payments to RTGS when RITS began real-time settlement in 1998. Low-value systems stayed on DNS but settle more often to shrink the gap. The NPP is the break from that pattern: retail payments settled on an RTGS basis around the clock. DNS obligations still settle in RITS, as net batch amounts across ESAs.

The RITS System Queue

In RITS, payments other than NPP payments don't settle the instant they arrive. They join the RITS System Queue, where each is tested before it settles:

  1. A payment is submitted, for example an HVCS payment through SWIFT, an Austraclear trade, or a batch's net obligation.
  2. It's tested: does the paying bank have enough in its RITS Allocation, and do its own payment controls allow it now?
  3. If yes, it settles gross and final across the two ESAs.
  4. If no, it waits and is retested as money arrives: incoming payments, or an intraday repo with the Reserve Bank.
  5. Two payments going in opposite directions between two banks can settle together, which frees up gridlock.
  6. Payments still unsettled at the end of the day are removed and must be resubmitted.

The queue lets payments wait briefly for incoming funds instead of failing, so banks need less money on hand.

Sources

The article says only that the FSS settles NPP payments independently of other RITS payments, which “are settled by the RITS System Queue”. The queue mechanics here (retesting, offsetting, end-of-day removal) are general knowledge of RITS and simplified. RITS has more detail, including how banks prioritise payments; check the Reserve Bank's RITS documentation for exact rules.

The FSS doesn't queue because a customer is waiting in their banking app: an immediate settle-or-reject answer keeps an NPP payment to seconds. That's why banks keep their FSS Allocation funded with trigger points (play 6).
